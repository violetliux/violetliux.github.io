Huddle

Built for the hesitant hello.

Huddle explores how college students can move from shared interests to a first conversation. Our team developed and tested a social-app prototype designed to make approaching others feel more comfortable.

ContextSCAD · UXDG 701, Theory of UX · Spring 2026
Project typeAcademic team project
StatusInteractive prototype
My roleUX Designer · UI & Interaction Focus

My contributionI collaborated across all four phases of the project, from research and problem definition to design development and testing. While my assigned role focused on UI and interaction design, our team shared responsibilities throughout the process.

  • Overview
  • Problem
  • Direction
  • Finder
  • Chat
  • Experience
  • Outcome
The starting point

Meeting people is easier than making the first move.

We wanted to understand what happens before a first conversation. In our interviews, students described fear of rejection and uncertainty about social norms. Our research also identified shared interests, backgrounds, and goals as ways people found something to talk about. That led us to design for a smaller first step, with shared-interest Spaces and prompts that give students a way to begin.

Starting feels risky

Fear of rejection made students hesitate to start a conversation.

EvidenceInterviews and our Five Whys traced students not starting conversations back to fear of rejection and unclear social norms.

Circles are already set

Established friend groups were hard to join, and online contact stayed shallow.

EvidenceObservations showed students watching more than engaging online and trading quick messages rather than conversations, while stable friend groups grew harder to enter.

Time runs out

Competing schedules left little room to turn an encounter into something ongoing.

EvidenceStudents described limited time as a reason encounters didn’t become ongoing relationships.

Huddle team research · 25 survey responses, 6 interviews, 6 observation sessions, and a diary study with 3 participants.

Design direction

We gave students context before asking them to connect.

What we learnedDecision it informed
Shared interests give people something to talk aboutDecision it informedMake interests visible through Spaces and profiles
Initiating can feel uncertainDecision it informedProvide conversation prompts
Participation requires planning and reassuranceDecision it informedShow event details and who is attending

One journey, from a shared interest to a first message.

Each step answers the question that stops someone at the one before it. A shared interest gives a reason to look at an event; the event shows who is going; a familiar face makes the first message easier to send. That is why Spaces, Events and Chats sit side by side in the bottom navigation.

From quick sketches to a testable prototype.

We sketched widely with Crazy 8s, told the story of a student going from alone to connected, and organised the app around it. Low-fi wireframes set the structure, and the mid-fi prototype is what we took into testing.

1 / 5Crazy 8s. Quick sketches to explore different design possibilities and features.

Main decision · Finder

Nearby wasn’t enough. Students needed context and control.

Finder

Give students more context before they reach out.

Initial idea

Help students discover people nearby, so an online connection could become a real-world one.

Testing feedback

In prototype testing, participants wanted more information about nearby people and clearer explanations of location sharing.

Our response

In the revised map, nearby people appear as larger profile photos and every tab in the navigation is labelled, so people are easier to recognise and it is clearer where you are. We kept the location prompt and profile preview as they were: the prompt already explained why Huddle asks for location, and the preview already showed someone’s interests before “Say hi!”.

The before/after pair shows the recognition and navigation changes. The location prompt and profile preview show where privacy and profile context appear in the flow.

Trade-off
  • Making someone easier to discover also raises the question of how visible they want to be. Larger photos help recognition, but they also make each person more prominent on the map.
Remaining question
  • Whether students understand the location-sharing choices and feel comfortable with them. We didn’t test that directly.
Supporting iteration · Chat

Making conversations easier to find.

Testing exposed a cluttered conversation list. We separated friends, groups and advisers so students could more easily find the conversation they needed.

Final experience

How the experience comes together.

Spaces, Events, Chats and Finder each carry one step of the same journey, from a shared interest to a first conversation.

Kingston Campass

Knowing the room number was only the beginning.

Kingston Campass explores the journey between knowing where a class is and finding the way there. Our team connected destination search, route choices and navigation guidance in a campus-app prototype.

ContextKingston University London · Design Marketing II · September–December 2024
Project typeAcademic team project
StatusInteractive prototype
My roleUX Designer · Shared Research & Design Responsibilities

My contributionI collaborated with two teammates throughout the project, from research and problem definition to interface design, prototyping, and user testing. We shared responsibilities across the process rather than working within fixed roles.

  • Overview
  • Problem
  • Design direction
  • Route choices
  • Directions
  • Final journey
  • Next step
The starting point

Finding the building was only part of finding the classroom.

After an initial survey, we interviewed Kingston students from different disciplines at Knights Park and Penrhyn Road. Students described difficulty finding classrooms and interpreting directions such as east and west. We connected campus maps with indoor guidance and used instructions such as left and right to describe the next action.

Finding the campus wasn’t finding the classroom.

Students across different disciplines described difficulty finding their classrooms once they reached a building.

Design implicationConnect campus maps with more detailed indoor guidance.

Cardinal directions were hard to interpret.

Directions such as east and west were difficult to follow while walking. Students wanted simpler guidance.

Design implicationUse left and right, distances and visual cues.

Routes had to fit different circumstances.

Students were interested in customised routes, accessibility options, weather and links to their class schedules.

Design implicationBring route preferences and timetable planning into one experience.

Research: a survey, then several rounds of interviews with students from different disciplines at Knights Park and Penrhyn Road.

Design direction

Connect a destination to an understandable next step.

The core journey links choosing a destination with comparing routes, following directions and arriving. Timetable entries and saved places give students other ways to start, so they don’t have to search for the same room every week.

How the team got from research to screens.

We planned the work over twelve weeks, wrote personas that included a wheelchair user, mapped how the app’s features connect, and wireframed the main screens.

1 / 4Project timeline. Twelve weeks, from the idea and research to UI design, prototyping and user testing.

The screens that connect those steps.

Main decision · Route choices

How much space should route choices take up?

While comparing journeys, students need both the route modes and the map. We designed two ways of offering the modes and tested both with students.

Option A

Compact dropdown.

Design shown

Leaves more room for route information. Students open the menu to see the available modes.

Option B

Persistent selector.

Design shown

Shows the modes together. The selector takes up space throughout route comparison.

Final design

Compact dropdown.

We carried this option forward to keep more of the map and route information visible.

Students tested both alternatives in the prototype before we carried the compact dropdown into the final interface.

Supporting decision · Directions

Directions needed to be easier to interpret.

Because cardinal directions were hard to follow, both navigation views lead with the next action and its distance. Map cards show the next turn and time remaining; the AR concept places the path and turn markers over the camera view.

Map and AR navigation concepts shown in the prototype.

Final experience

The final campus journey.

Destination search, route options, the daily planner and campus shortcuts in one app.

Outcome

Interface testing was the first step.

We produced a clickable prototype and tested it in two rounds with students from Kingston and other universities: first navigation, widgets and settings, then the visual interface, onboarding and how features connect.

Tasks tested
  • Choose a destination and explore route options.
  • Navigate to a destination and save or share the route.
  • Use and edit home-screen widgets.
  • Explore settings and personalization.
  • Follow the introductory tutorial.

Testing takeawayFeedback identified useful elements, including destination search and map interactions, while also revealing areas that needed clearer guidance, stronger visual hierarchy, and better control of map visibility.

Testing covered the interface and proposed interactions. It did not verify real-world route accuracy, AR performance or accessibility.

Next step

Watch students follow complete routes on campus, supported by verified entrance, lift and closure information.

Ornata

What does this pattern mean?

Ornata began with a detail I noticed during museum visits: labels explained the objects, but often said little about their decorative patterns. I explored how visitors could uncover those stories and keep the patterns that interested them.

ContextAcademic project · Kingston University · 2025
Project typeIndividual project
StatusApp prototype & AR demonstration
My roleResearch, concept, app design, illustration & campaign

My contributionAn individual project. I carried out the research and developed the concept, the app design and prototype, the illustrations and the proposed campaign.

  • Overview
  • Origin
  • Design direction
  • Categories
  • Creation
  • Beyond the museum
  • Next step
The starting point

Visitors could see the patterns. Finding their stories took more work.

During visits to the British Museum, I noticed that labels often explained the object while giving less detail about its decorative patterns. In my survey, difficulty finding relevant content was the most frequently selected barrier to exploring cultural heritage or traditional designs. That gave me a starting point for Ornata: help visitors explore the pattern already in front of them.

What gets in the way of exploring cultural heritage?

Audience research pointed the same way.

Familiar motifs, unfamiliar stories

People recognised traditional motifs without necessarily knowing their significance.

Design responseConnect each motif with its origin and meaning.

Relevant information was hard to find

Interviewees wanted something quick to use, and survey responses pointed to difficulty finding relevant content.

Design responseMake the pattern itself the way in.

People wanted to keep what interested them

Some interviewees wanted to save favourite patterns and organise them into folders.

Design responsePut saving and organising alongside discovery.

Research: interviews, a survey, museum visits and secondary research on cultural engagement and digital experiences.

Design direction

Start with the object already in view.

I made scanning a proposed entry point to pattern information. Visitors could start with an object that caught their attention, explore the motif’s story, and save it to revisit later. Scanning is shown through an AR demonstration in the prototype; I didn’t build a working recognition system.

Creating a personal pattern comes after this core journey, as an extension for people who want to keep exploring, rather than as a feature with equal priority.

From pattern research to a working flow.

I studied the patterns and the objects they come from, mapped the flow from noticing an object to saving its story, worked out the screens in mid-fi wireframes, and drew the illustrations that later replaced the category textures.

1 / 4Pattern research. Reference objects, a redrawn motif and its colour palette.

Main decision · Browsing categories

A category name needed more context.

Categories

Give visitors something to go on before they choose.

Before

Category names and textures gave visitors few clues about what they would find.

After

Illustrated cards pair each object with a short description and an arrow to its content. On the homepage, illustrations replace the textures for the four browsing routes: dynasty, ethnic groups, region and objects.

Category illustrations bring visual context to the four browsing routes: dynasty, ethnic groups, region, and objects.

The object list also changed between these versions, so the comparison shows a revised set of categories as well as a new card design.

Trade-off
  • The cards take more space, so fewer categories fit on screen, but each gives more information before someone taps.
Validation
  • This change came from my own interface comparisons rather than participant feedback, and I haven’t retested it with visitors.
Supporting iteration · Creation

Creation revealed a different kind of uncertainty.

Participants completed all four prototype tasks: signing in, using AR, scanning and designing a pattern. Designing took longest, about 40 seconds, and participants hesitated over colours and shapes and asked for more adjustment options. I added exposure, contrast, saturation and temperature to the size and rotation controls.

Create your own pattern

More controls, and an open question.

I didn’t retest whether the extra controls resolved the hesitation. More options may help people express themselves, but they also add complexity to a step that already made people pause.

Final experience & proposed campaign

Carrying discovery beyond the museum.

Saved patterns, visual storytelling and physical products explored ways for a museum encounter to continue beyond the visit. The partnerships and campaign shown here are proposals.

Prototype

Suggested task

Good to know

A concept prototype with limited working paths.

Open prototype ↗ Watch a short walkthrough ↗
Museum campaign

Introduce Ornata where the discovery begins.

I proposed museum partnerships that would introduce Ornata through exhibitions. Pattern-led posters and exhibition-specific collecting activities would connect the app with objects visitors could encounter in person.

Social storytelling

Give a pattern a story worth following.

I developed social content using artifact imagery, pattern details, and a notebook-inspired format. The proposed approach introduced cultural stories through visual details and invited audiences to continue exploring through Ornata.

Physical outcomes

Bring the patterns into everyday objects.

I extended the visual work into printed stickers and silk scarves, exploring how the reinterpreted patterns could exist beyond the screen.

Physical design outcomes created for the project.

Outcome

A broad concept with a focused next step.

Ornata produced an app prototype, an AR demonstration of scanning, a proposed museum campaign, and printed and woven pattern pieces. It was explored through research and prototype testing; live museum use, adoption and long-term engagement were not measured.

Next step

Test whether visitors can move from noticing a pattern to finding useful information during an actual museum visit.

Depends on
  • Recognition that works reliably on real artifacts, under gallery lighting
Depends on
  • Accurate, sourced cultural content, developed with curators and specialists

Baker’s Pride

Designing a sweeter ordering experience.

A custom cake starts with an idea, but communicating that idea can involve phone calls, reference photos and several conversations. Our team redesigned Baker’s Pride’s website to help customers explore their options and assemble a clearer cake request for the bakery to review.

ContextSCAD · UXDG 731, Information Architecture for Designers · Fall 2026
Project typeAcademic team redesign of an existing bakery website
StatusInteractive website prototype
My roleUX Designer · Shared Research & Design Responsibilities

My contributionI collaborated with two teammates across research, information architecture, interface design, prototyping, and usability testing. We shared responsibilities throughout the project.

  • Overview
  • Starting point
  • Direction
  • Labels
  • Cake request
  • Iteration
  • Final
  • Outcome
The starting point

One cake request could pass through several conversations.

Customers usually started a custom cake request by phone or at the counter. Staff helped them choose a size and flavour, while reference photos could arrive separately by email. One staff member saw value in bringing those details together. Another described how difficult it had been to manage online orders alongside walk-ins and phone calls.

How cake details reached the bakery.

Research: in-store observation, four customer interviews, three staff interviews and competitor research.

Defining the direction

Support browsing and purposeful visits.

Some customers wanted to look around before deciding. Others arrived with an occasion and a cake in mind. We explored a browsing-led approach and a task-led approach, then brought both entry points into the final concept.

Customer intentionEntry point
“I want to see what they have.”Entry pointBakery Menu and Seasonal & New
“I need a cake for an occasion.”Entry pointCustom Cakes and Start a Cake Request
“I’m planning a visit.”Entry pointVisit & Contact

From site map to final concept.

We reorganised the site map, compared a browsing-led and a task-led concept, tested the idea with a storyboard, and combined both entry points in the final concept before building mid-fi wireframes.

1 / 6As-is site map. Cakes were split between occasion pages and a separate product list.

Supporting decision · Information architecture

Name sections around what customers need.

We replaced broad or ambiguous labels with clearer destinations. “Bakery Menu” tells visitors where to browse products, while “Custom Cakes” identifies a specific service. “Seasonal & New” gives changing offers a place beyond a single holiday.

Research noteAI-assisted label exploration

We also asked synthetic users, AI personas prompted with the site, to interpret our labels. They flagged ambiguity in “Our Goodies”, “Wedding” and “Contact Us”, which helped us word alternatives. Their scores (6/9 for the original labels, 9/9 for revised ones) are an AI-based clarity check, not evidence of improved usability for real customers.

Main decision · Cake request

Bring the request together, then leave room for review.

We brought the details and reference image into one guided request. Customers could review their selections before sending them, and bakery staff could check availability and design feasibility before confirming.

Break the request into guided steps

Occasion and date, size and flavour, design and photo, contact, then review.

Why it mattersOrganises the details customers need to provide.

Keep a visible “Your cake” summary

Every choice so far, with pickup details and an estimate, stays beside the form.

Why it mattersLets customers check their selections as they go.

End with “Request sent for review”

The last screen says the bakery will check the details and call to confirm.

Why it mattersSeparates submitting an idea from securing an order.

Trade-off

Staff review adds a confirmation step for the customer, but avoids treating every requested design or pickup date as automatically available.

This is the reasoning behind the proposal. We didn’t establish that the bakery would adopt it or that staff workload would fall.

Testing & iteration

Completing the task didn’t mean every step was clear.

Four participants tested the prototype through moderated, think-aloud tasks. Their hesitations still revealed places where the interface suggested the wrong next action.

Two changes made after testing.

Example A · Request flow

Keep the choice the customer already made.

What we saw

Participants could complete the cake request, but the flow asked about the occasion again after they had already selected a category.

What we changed

We retained that choice and made choosing a cake style the focus of the next screen.

Example B · Homepage

The homepage preview looked editable.

What we saw

Participants mistook the sample cake-request details on the homepage for an editable form.

What we changed

We removed the sample answers and simplified the overview beside the request button.

These are changes made after testing. We didn’t run another round, so they aren’t proven improvements.

Final experience

From browsing to a clearer request.

The final prototype brings product browsing, seasonal offerings, practical visit information and custom-cake requests into one experience. Customers can explore before deciding, then gather the details needed to discuss a cake with the bakery.

Outcome

A clearer customer journey, with operational questions still open.

We developed an interactive redesign and evaluated it with four participants. Testing showed that participants could complete the selected journeys, while also revealing misleading cues and repeated questions. The resulting revisions addressed those issues; adoption, staff workload and business impact were not measured.

Violet, on paper.

The personal brand system behind this portfolio: a vintage, minimal stationery look built on one violet palette, Libre Baskerville with DM Sans, and a few paper objects used with restraint.

Principles

Paper, not screen

Warm paper ground, hairline rules and printed-matter details (tabs, stamps, tape) make the site feel collected and personal.

Quiet, then one flourish

Each section has at most one decorative object. Everything around it stays plain so the work leads.

Evidence first

Numbers, methods and outcomes are set larger than adjectives. Recruiters should find impact in five seconds.

Brand mark

Your illustrated avatar is the logo: it sits beside the name in the navigation at 36px, always as a circle. Keep clear space of half the logo's width on every side. Decoration is editorial and print-inspired, never cute: a registration mark separates words in the serif bands, and line-drawn desk and UI objects (paper clip, cursor, locate target, compass, wax seal, postage stamp) sit on the paper.

Motion & access

Do
  • Keep text at 4.5:1 or better; use the table above.
  • Show a 2px Japanese Violet focus ring on every control.
  • Use 0.2s colour fades and the ticker as the only moving parts.
  • Write alt text for every case study image.
Don't
  • Put Old Lavender body text on Lavender Gray or Thistle.
  • Use more than one decorative object per section.
  • Tilt or tape case study images; keep work straight and readable.
  • Animate anything when reduced motion is on.
